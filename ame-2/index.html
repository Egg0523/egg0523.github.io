Attention-Based Perceptive Locomotion

A perceptive locomotion policy for an armed humanoid, operating on a map it builds itself.

AgiBot · Motion Intelligence Group · 2026 · Isaac Lab, simulation

Overview

A perceptive locomotion policy has to select footholds from a map it builds itself, most of which is irrelevant at any given step. An attention module weights local map features against a query assembled from the robot’s own state, concentrating the network on the terrain relevant to the next foothold. The mapping pipeline projects depth images into an elevation grid carrying a per-cell uncertainty, so occlusions are represented as unknown rather than as confident flat ground.

Trained in Isaac Lab for the X2: a higher centre of mass, a smaller support polygon and swinging arms relative to the quadrupeds this class of policy is usually built for.

Method

  • Attention map encoding — a query formed from global map features and proprioception weights the local map features, so the policy attends to the terrain under the next foothold rather than consuming the map uniformly.
  • Uncertainty channel — depth images are projected into a local elevation grid carrying height and confidence per cell, fused into a common frame through odometry. Occluded cells reach the policy marked unknown.
  • Teacher–student distillation — the teacher trains on ground-truth elevation maps; the student receives only its own online map and a window of recent state, matching the teacher’s actions while continuing to improve under its own reward.
  • Rates — policy at 50 Hz, joint targets tracked at 400 Hz.
  • Terrain curriculum — dense, climbing and sparse families, promoted and demoted on measured success.

In simulation

The policy traverses stepping stones, stairs and slopes in both directions, and discrete raised platforms, arriving at each goal on the commanded heading.

Left, the elevation map the policy operates on, built from the robot’s own depth images; right, the same run in Isaac Lab. Hover or tap to play.

Heading is the non-trivial part. On sparse footholds there is no continuous ground on which to turn in place, so orientation has to emerge from foothold selection itself: which stones are chosen, in what order, and how the turn is distributed across them.

The same policy running in MuJoCo, elevation map again on the left.

Results

On unseen test terrain: 90% success as teacher, 75% as student.

The gap between the two is the cost of perception. The teacher plans on ground truth; the student plans on a map built from its own depth images, and mapping error surfaces as missed footholds. Improving mapping under occlusion is therefore the higher-return direction.

Retraining the student

The student was then retrained with an explicit upper-body posture constraint. Across the same terrain the resulting gait is a clear improvement on the first version’s teacher.

The retrained student crossing pyramid stairs, a discrete raised-block field, gravel and a stair maze.

Failure analysis on stairs

Ascending consecutive steps, foot placement drifted progressively further off. The cause was not the controller: the local elevation map is fused into a global frame through odometry, and odometry drift displaced the map relative to the real staircase, so feet were placed accurately onto step edges that were no longer where the policy believed them to be. Descent produced a related gait instability.

An armed humanoid also required its own termination conditions and terrain curriculum; the thresholds that define a fall for a quadruped do not transfer.
