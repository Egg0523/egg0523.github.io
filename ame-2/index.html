Attention-Based Perceptive Locomotion

Reproducing AME-2 and moving it off the quadruped it was built for, onto an armed humanoid.

AgiBot · Motion Intelligence Group · 2026 · Isaac Lab, simulation

Zhang et al., AME-2 Project page

Overview

A perceptive locomotion policy has to decide where to put its feet from a map it built itself, and most of that map is irrelevant at any given step. AME-2 makes the policy choose what to look at: an attention module weights local map features against a query assembled from the robot's own state, so the network concentrates on the patch of ground that actually matters for the next foothold. Underneath it, a learned mapping pipeline turns depth images into an elevation grid that carries a per-cell uncertainty, so occlusions are represented as uncertainty rather than as confident flat ground.

The published results are on a quadruped and a point-foot biped. I reproduced the method in Isaac Lab and transferred it to the X2 humanoid — a taller centre of mass, a smaller support polygon, arms that swing.

How it works

  • Attention map encoding — a CNN produces pointwise local features; a pooled global descriptor and the proprioception embedding form the query; multi-head attention weights the local features against it, and the result conditions the action decoder.
  • Uncertainty-aware mapping — depth images are projected into a local elevation grid whose cells carry both a height and a confidence, fused into a common frame through odometry.
  • Teacher–student distillation — a teacher trains with ground-truth elevation maps under PPO; the student sees only the online-built map plus a temporal encoding of its own history, and is trained with PPO alongside action-distillation and representation-alignment losses.
  • Rates — the policy runs at 50 Hz and its joint targets are tracked at 400 Hz.
  • Terrain curriculum — dense, climbing and sparse families, promoted and demoted on measured success.

In simulation

Crossing open ground onto a discrete stepping-block field, with the gravel patch and pyramid stairs in frame. Hover or tap to play.

Results

On unseen test terrain the reproduced policy reaches 90% success as a teacher and 75% as a student, against 95.2% and 82.4% for the paper's quadruped.

The number worth reading is not either figure on its own but the distance between them. The published teacher-to-student drop is 12.8 points, and it exists because the student navigates on a map it built from depth rather than on the ground truth. My drop is about fifteen points — close enough to the original that the loss is attributable to the change of embodiment rather than to something broken in the reimplementation, which is the question a reproduction has to answer about itself.

Moving it onto a humanoid

The most instructive failure was on stairs. Climbing several steps in sequence, the policy's foot placement drifted a little further off with each one. The cause was not the controller: the local elevation map is fused into a global frame through odometry, and as odometry drifted the map slid relative to the real staircase, so the policy was placing feet accurately onto step edges that were no longer where it believed them to be. Descending stairs produced a related gait instability.

Alongside that, moving from the paper's platforms to an armed humanoid meant revisiting the termination conditions and the terrain curriculum — the thresholds that define a fall for a quadruped do not describe one for a biped carrying arms.
