Attention-Based Perceptive Locomotion

Teaching an armed humanoid where to put its feet, from a map it builds itself.

AgiBot · Motion Intelligence Group · 2026 · Isaac Lab, simulation

Overview

A perceptive locomotion policy has to decide where to put its feet using a map it built itself, and most of that map is irrelevant at any given step. This one learns what to look at: an attention module weights local map features against a query assembled from the robot's own state, so the network concentrates on the patch of ground that matters for the next foothold. Underneath it, the mapping pipeline turns depth images into an elevation grid carrying a per-cell uncertainty, so an occlusion is represented as something unknown rather than as confident flat ground.

I trained it in Isaac Lab for the X2 — a taller centre of mass than the quadrupeds this class of policy is usually built for, a smaller support polygon, and arms that swing.

How it works

  • Attention map encoding — conditioned on its own state, the policy learns which part of the map to attend to instead of consuming all of it uniformly.
  • Uncertainty-aware mapping — depth images are projected into a local elevation grid whose cells carry both a height and a confidence, fused into a common frame through odometry.
  • Teacher–student distillation — a teacher trains against ground-truth elevation maps; the student sees only the map it builds online, and learns to match the teacher while still improving on its own.
  • Rates — the policy runs at 50 Hz, with its joint targets tracked at 400 Hz.
  • Terrain curriculum — dense, climbing and sparse families, promoted and demoted on measured success.

In simulation

Crossing open ground onto a discrete stepping-block field, with the gravel patch and pyramid stairs in frame. Hover or tap to play.

Results

On unseen test terrain the policy reaches 90% success as a teacher and 75% as a student.

The gap between those two is the interesting part. The teacher plans on ground truth; the student plans on a map it built from its own depth images, and everything it gets wrong about that map shows up as a missed foothold. Fifteen points is what the perception costs, and it is the number to attack next — better mapping under occlusion buys more here than a better policy would.

What went wrong on stairs

The most instructive failure was on stairs. Climbing several steps in sequence, the policy's foot placement drifted a little further off with each one. The cause was not the controller: the local elevation map is fused into a global frame through odometry, and as odometry drifted the map slid relative to the real staircase, so the policy was placing feet accurately onto step edges that were no longer where it believed them to be. Descending stairs produced a related gait instability.

Alongside that, an armed humanoid needed its own termination conditions and terrain curriculum: the thresholds that define a fall for a quadruped do not describe one for a biped carrying arms.
