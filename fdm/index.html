Perceptive Forward-Dynamics Navigation

A local planner that learns what the robot can actually do, running on the X2 humanoid.

AgiBot · Motion Intelligence Group · 2026 · Deployed on hardware

Overview

A local planner normally needs a traversability cost map, and that map needs retuning for every new environment. This one replaces the map with a learned model of the robot itself: given recent proprioception, a height scan of the surrounding terrain and a candidate sequence of velocity commands, it predicts where the robot will actually end up over the next five seconds and how likely that sequence is to end in a fall or a collision. A sampling planner scores hundreds of candidate sequences against those predictions and executes the best one.

Because the model is trained on trajectories from this robot running this locomotion policy, its predictions carry what the machine can really do rather than a rigid-body approximation.

The pages below follow how it got onto the robot: first in simulation, then on hardware with perception and planning still running off-board over a cable, and finally with the cable gone and the whole chain on the robot’s own compute.

How it works

  • Inputs — proprioceptive history (base velocities, projected gravity, joint states, previous actions) plus a height scan of the local terrain.
  • Residual prediction — the model does not regress poses. It predicts the gap between the velocity you asked for and the velocity the robot actually achieves, and integrates that into five seconds of motion. Learning a correction to a simple integrator is a far easier target than learning the trajectory outright, and it keeps the output physically consistent by construction.
  • A second head for risk — a separate output emits a per-step failure probability, so “where will I be” and “will this end badly” are answered by the same forward pass.
  • Planning — a sampling planner rolls candidate command sequences through the model and scores them on two terms only: distance to goal, and predicted risk. No hand-authored cost map anywhere in the loop.
  • Risk across neighbours — the risk term accumulates over adjacent sampled paths rather than judging each in isolation, so one optimistic prediction cannot clear a whole fan of commands. This is what makes the planner wait rather than squeeze past.

In simulation

Everything was brought up in simulation first, where the robot can be asked to walk into things and nothing breaks. The clip below runs the physics rollout beside the planner view: the fan of sampled command sequences, each one scored by the model, and the trajectory that wins.

The planner’s map view on the left — obstacles, the robot’s pose and the sampled trajectories — and the simulator it is driving on the right. Hover or tap to play.

There is a structural difficulty with moving obstacles here. The height scan is a single snapshot, so a five-second rollout implicitly assumes the world holds still for five seconds — which is exactly what a person walking across the corridor does not do. Closing a perception–replanning loop at 10 Hz is what makes the assumption survivable: the plan is never more than a tenth of a second old.

Against a 1 m/s obstacle inside 4 m, the planner clears it every time in simulation.

On hardware, tethered

The first hardware bring-up kept perception and planning on an off-board machine, with the robot on a cable. That is the arrangement in both clips below — the tether is the loop of cable trailing across the floor.

Navigating to a commanded goal. The monitor at the start shows the elevation map the planner is working from. Hover or tap to play.

The robot clears doorways, pillar fields and corridors on flat ground, and reaches goals up to 10 m away within 0.3 m of position error.

The same planner with a person stepping into its path. Hover or tap to play.

Here the same 1 m/s obstacle is cleared 80% of the time. The gap to simulation is latency: between the depth frame arriving and the motors acting on the replanned command, the obstacle has already moved, and the plan the robot executes is a plan for a world a moment out of date.

The other 20% is not a collision. The robot stops and waits for the obstacle to pass. Across both settings there were no collisions at all.

That waiting behaviour is a consequence of the cost design rather than a bug. Risk is accumulated across neighbouring sampled paths on purpose, so that a single missed collision prediction cannot slip through. When something crosses the corridor the whole sampled fan goes high-risk at once, no candidate clears the threshold, and the planner settles on near-zero velocity until the way is clear. Loosening that term buys shorter paths and costs safety — for a humanoid on hardware I would rather it wait.

On hardware, untethered

Then the cable came off. Perception and planning moved onto the computer the robot carries, and what still goes over the air is the operator’s link rather than the control loop. Two things change with that. The whole chain now has to fit inside the compute the robot has and still close at rate — there is no bigger machine to fall back on. And the robot is no longer working inside the radius of a cable, so it can be sent at a goal anywhere in the room and walk through whatever happens to be in the way.

Carrying the semantic layer onto the robot

The FDM also serves as the action expert for the layered navigation stack. On the robot the semantic layer runs on Qwen-3-8B-VL: it reads the instruction together with the current camera view and returns a goal as a pixel coordinate. That point is projected into the robot’s body frame as a waypoint and handed to the FDM, which turns it into velocity commands the machine can actually execute.

The division of labour is deliberate. The language model decides where to go and is allowed to be slow about it; nothing it emits is trusted to be reachable. Everything about whether the robot can get there — what the ground looks like, what the body will do if asked, when to wait — stays with the planner underneath, which already runs at rate and is already verified on this robot.

3-D terrain, and what the planner does not know

What this model predicts is a path and a probability of failing. Neither of those says anything about feet. Point it at a field of stepping stones and it will find a way across the field — but it has no representation of where a foot should land, and getting across at all is entirely the work of the locomotion policy underneath it.

Crossing a stepping-stone field, with the elevation map on the left. Hover or tap to play.

Watch the stones closely and the robot sometimes walks between the posts rather than over their tops. The path was fine. The footholds were never part of the question being asked, so nothing in this layer was ever going to choose them.

That gap is why I went and trained the locomotion policy on the next page. It puts foothold selection inside the network, so the robot is picking where to step instead of surviving whatever the path hands it.

Notes

  • A humanoid carries a higher centre of mass, a smaller support polygon and more degrees of freedom than the quadrupeds this class of planner is usually built for, so the observation space, command sampling ranges and termination conditions all needed revisiting.
  • The model's predictions are tied to the locomotion policy underneath it: change that policy and the trajectories have to be re-collected and the model retrained. The two layers cannot be swapped independently.
