Perceptive Forward-Dynamics Navigation

Reproducing a learned perceptive FDM on the AgiBot X2 humanoid, putting it on hardware, and taking it past what the paper covered.

AgiBot · Motion Intelligence Group · 2026 · Deployed on hardware

Roth et al., RSS 2025 Original code

Overview

A local planner usually needs a traversability cost map, and that map needs tuning for every new environment. The learned perceptive Forward Dynamics Model replaces it: given the robot's recent proprioception, a height scan of the surrounding geometry, and a candidate sequence of velocity commands, it predicts where the robot will actually end up over the next five seconds and how likely that sequence is to end in failure. An MPPI planner samples command sequences, scores them with those two predictions, and executes the best one.

The published work was built on the ANYmal quadruped. I migrated it to the X2 humanoid, brought the full perception–planning–control pipeline up on the real robot, and then pushed into a case the paper explicitly lists as untested.

How it works

  • Inputs — proprioceptive history (base velocities, projected gravity, joint states, previous actions) plus a height scan of the local terrain.
  • Prediction — ten future SE(2) poses at 0.5 s spacing, a five-second horizon, with a failure probability attached to each step.
  • Planning — MPPI samples command sequences, rolls each through the model, and scores them on distance to goal plus predicted risk. No hand-authored cost map anywhere in the loop.
  • Platform awareness — the model is trained on trajectories from one specific robot running one specific locomotion policy, so its predictions carry that platform's real capabilities rather than a rigid-body approximation.

Running on the X2

MuJoCo rollout beside the RViz planner view — sampled trajectories through a pillar field. Hover or tap to play.

On hardware the robot traverses doorways, ascending and descending stairs, pillar fields and corridors, and reaches 4 m goals within 0.3 m of position error.

Beyond the paper: moving obstacles

The paper's limitations are explicit on this point — the method was not tested with fast-moving objects or multiple dynamic agents. That is a real gap rather than an oversight: the height scan is a single snapshot, so a five-second rollout implicitly assumes the world holds still for five seconds.

Against a 1 m/s moving obstacle, a 10 Hz perception–replanning loop clears it 80% of the time. The other 20% is not a collision: the robot stops and waits for the obstacle to pass. Across the runs there were no collisions at all.

That failure mode follows from the reward formulation rather than from a bug. The risk term sums predicted failure probability across neighbouring sampled paths, deliberately, so that one missed collision prediction cannot slip through. When something crosses the corridor the whole sampled fan goes high-risk at once, no trajectory clears the threshold, and the planner settles on near-zero velocity until the way is clear again. The paper's own ablation shows the same trade in miniature: removing the risk term shortens path times and lowers success rates.

Carrying the semantic layer onto the robot

The FDM also serves as the action expert for the layered navigation stack. The VLM planner emits a goal as a pixel coordinate in the current camera view; that goal is projected into the robot's body frame as a waypoint and handed to the FDM, which turns it into safe velocity commands.

The dual-system paper pairs its planner with an RGB-only diffusion policy, and its ablation shows that swapping in a modular point-goal planner costs accuracy. The reason to do it anyway is that the RGB policy has never seen the X2, while the FDM has an elevation-map view of what is traversable and a sim-to-real transfer that is already verified on this robot. It is a trade of benchmark accuracy for hardware safety, made knowingly.

Notes on the transfer

  • The published model runs on a quadruped; the X2 is a humanoid with a higher centre of mass, a smaller support polygon and more degrees of freedom, so the observation space, command sampling ranges and termination conditions all needed revisiting.
  • Because the model's predictions are tied to the locomotion policy underneath it, changing that policy means re-collecting trajectories and retraining — the two layers cannot be swapped independently.
