Perceptive Forward-Dynamics Navigation

A local planner that learns what the robot can actually do, running on the X2 humanoid.

AgiBot · Motion Intelligence Group · 2026 · Deployed on hardware

Overview

A local planner normally needs a traversability cost map, and that map needs retuning for every new environment. This one replaces the map with a learned model of the robot itself: given recent proprioception, a height scan of the surrounding terrain and a candidate sequence of velocity commands, it predicts where the robot will actually end up over the next five seconds and how likely that sequence is to end in a fall or a collision. A sampling planner scores hundreds of candidate sequences against those predictions and executes the best one.

Because the model is trained on trajectories from this robot running this locomotion policy, its predictions carry what the machine can really do rather than a rigid-body approximation. I brought the whole perception–planning–control pipeline up on the X2 and then took it into moving-obstacle scenes.

How it works

  • Inputs — proprioceptive history (base velocities, projected gravity, joint states, previous actions) plus a height scan of the local terrain.
  • Residual prediction — the model does not regress poses. It predicts the gap between the velocity you asked for and the velocity the robot actually achieves, and integrates that into five seconds of motion. Learning a correction to a simple integrator is a far easier target than learning the trajectory outright, and it keeps the output physically consistent by construction.
  • A second head for risk — a separate output emits a per-step failure probability, so “where will I be” and “will this end badly” are answered by the same forward pass.
  • Planning — a sampling planner rolls candidate command sequences through the model and scores them on two terms only: distance to goal, and predicted risk. No hand-authored cost map anywhere in the loop.
  • Risk across neighbours — the risk term accumulates over adjacent sampled paths rather than judging each in isolation, so one optimistic prediction cannot clear a whole fan of commands. This is what makes the planner wait rather than squeeze past.

Moving obstacles

There is a structural difficulty here. The height scan is a single snapshot, so a five-second rollout implicitly assumes the world holds still for five seconds — which is exactly what a person walking across the corridor does not do.

In simulation, a 10 Hz perception–replanning loop clears a 1 m/s obstacle inside 4 m every time. On the real robot the same planner clears it 80% of the time. The gap is latency: between the depth frame arriving and the motors acting on the replanned command, the obstacle has already moved, and the plan the robot executes is a plan for a world a moment out of date.

The other 20% is not a collision. The robot stops and waits for the obstacle to pass. Across both settings there were no collisions at all.

That waiting behaviour is a consequence of the cost design rather than a bug. Risk is accumulated across neighbouring sampled paths on purpose, so that a single missed collision prediction cannot slip through. When something crosses the corridor the whole sampled fan goes high-risk at once, no candidate clears the threshold, and the planner settles on near-zero velocity until the way is clear. Loosening that term buys shorter paths and costs safety — for a humanoid on hardware I would rather it wait.

Carrying the semantic layer onto the robot

The FDM also serves as the action expert for the layered navigation stack. The VLM planner emits a goal as a pixel coordinate in the current camera view; that goal is projected into the robot's body frame as a waypoint and handed to the FDM, which turns it into safe velocity commands.

Routing the goals through this planner rather than through the RGB trajectory network costs some accuracy on the benchmark. It buys something worth more on hardware: an elevation-map view of what is traversable, and a transfer to this robot that is already verified. That trade was made knowingly.

Notes

  • A humanoid carries a higher centre of mass, a smaller support polygon and more degrees of freedom than the quadrupeds this class of planner is usually built for, so the observation space, command sampling ranges and termination conditions all needed revisiting.
  • The model's predictions are tied to the locomotion policy underneath it: change that policy and the trajectories have to be re-collected and the model retrained. The two layers cannot be swapped independently.
