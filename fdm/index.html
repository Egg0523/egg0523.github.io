Perceptive Forward-Dynamics Navigation on a Humanoid

A learned-dynamics local planner for the AgiBot X2 humanoid.

AgiBot · Motion Intelligence Group · 2026 · Deployed on hardware

Overview

A local planner built on a learned model of the robot rather than a traversability cost map that needs retuning per environment. Given proprioceptive history, a local height scan and a candidate sequence of velocity commands, the model predicts the resulting motion over a 5 s horizon together with a per-step failure probability. A sampling planner scores several hundred candidates on two terms — distance to goal and predicted risk — and executes the best.

Training trajectories come from this robot under this locomotion policy, so the predictions reflect the platform’s actual capability rather than a rigid-body approximation.

Method

  • Inputs — proprioceptive history (base velocity, projected gravity, joint states, previous actions) and a local terrain height scan.
  • Residual prediction — the model predicts the residual between commanded and achieved velocity and integrates it into a 5 s trajectory, rather than regressing pose directly. This simplifies the learning target and keeps the output physically consistent by construction.
  • Risk head — a separate output emits a per-step failure probability from the same forward pass.
  • Planning — MPPI over sampled command sequences, scored on goal distance and predicted risk only. No hand-authored cost map in the loop.
  • Risk aggregation — risk accumulates across neighbouring sampled paths, so a single optimistic prediction cannot clear an entire fan of candidates.

In simulation

The planner was developed and evaluated in simulation first. Against a 1 m/s obstacle within 4 m, a 10 Hz perception–replanning loop achieves 100% avoidance.

Left, the planner map view — obstacles, robot pose and sampled trajectories; right, the simulator it drives. Hover or tap to play.

The height scan is a single snapshot, so a 5 s rollout assumes a static world. The replanning rate bounds how stale that assumption can become.

On hardware, tethered

First hardware deployment, with perception and planning on an off-board machine over a cable. On flat ground the robot clears doorways, pillar fields and corridors, reaching goals up to 10 m away within 0.3 m of position error.

Navigation to a commanded goal; the monitor shows the elevation map in use.
The same planner with a person entering its path.

The same 1 m/s obstacle is avoided 80% of the time. The gap to simulation is sensing-to-actuation latency: the plan being executed corresponds to a world state already out of date.

The remaining 20% is the planner holding position rather than colliding. Risk aggregation drives the entire sampled fan above threshold, and velocity settles near zero until the path clears. No collisions occurred in either setting.

On hardware, untethered

Perception and planning were then moved onto the robot’s onboard compute and the cable removed; the wireless link carries operator commands rather than the control loop. The full pipeline has to close at rate within the onboard compute budget, and the working range is no longer bounded by the tether.

Semantic layer

The planner also serves as the action expert for the layered navigation stack. The semantic layer runs Qwen-3-8B-VL: it takes the instruction and the current camera view and returns a goal as a pixel coordinate, which is projected into the body frame as a waypoint and passed to the planner.

The separation is deliberate. The language model determines the goal, is not required to run at control rate, and its output is not assumed reachable. Traversability, platform capability and timing remain with the planner beneath it.

Limits on 3-D terrain

The model predicts a path and a failure probability; neither encodes foot placement. Across a stepping-stone field the planner produces a viable route, but traversal itself is entirely the responsibility of the locomotion policy beneath it — the robot frequently passes between posts rather than stepping on them.

Notes

  • Relative to the quadrupeds this class of planner is usually built for, the humanoid has a higher centre of mass, a smaller support polygon and more degrees of freedom; observation space, command sampling ranges and termination conditions were revised accordingly.
  • The model is coupled to the locomotion policy beneath it: changing that policy requires re-collecting trajectories and retraining. The two layers cannot be swapped independently.
